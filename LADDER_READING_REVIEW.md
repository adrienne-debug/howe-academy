# Read and answer — a reading ladder for every kid (draft for review)

**Nothing here is loaded or live yet.** These are new files on a branch. No notebook prints them, no device sees them, and nothing was written to the family database. They are for you to read first, the way the boys' banks were.

This is the first of the five shared ladders in the notebooks study (§25, "units to develop" #1): **a short passage with a few questions**, in seven levels from 1st grade to 7th–8th. A child starts where a short placement puts them, keeps their place, and moves **up** at the end of a level instead of starting over. Every level written once serves every child after — Lucy, then Julian; Ellis climbing into what Lincoln reads; another family's 7th grader.

Why it matters now: Lucy's notebook has not one sentence to read, and her first test (May / summer 2027) has a reading part. Ellis lost his daily passage when his question bank replaced it. Lincoln gets one passage a week.

**What changed after your answers (11/23):** g1 and g2 now have **30 passages each** (20 new in each). Every g1 question and choice is rewritten in words as easy as the passage, so Lucy reads the whole page herself. From 3rd grade, every question is marked for "write first" days. Every passage has topic tags a family can switch off. New passages add North Carolina, snakes, dinosaurs and frogs. The six drier pieces are swapped for livelier ones of the same kind. All of it had a second read. The full list is under "Decided 11/23" near the end.

What I kept to, from your rules:
- **The answer never shows on the child's page.** Each question has the right letter and a one-line *why* for **your** page only — it points to the words in the passage that prove the answer.
- **Works printed and on a tablet.** Plain text, no pictures needed, no step that depends on covering anything. Circle a letter on paper; tap it on the tablet.
- **Any family can use it.** All writing is new (not copied from any book, test or workbook). No family names (none of any enrolled child's name, in any of the three families), no brands or characters. Topics: animals and nature, weather and space, how everyday things work, kids solving small problems, long-ago people and inventions, places, North Carolina.
- **About half stories, half true facts**, plus how-tos, letters or notices, and poems for variety (110 in all: 39 stories, 50 true-facts pieces, 8 how-tos, 7 poems, 6 letters or notices).
- **True facts only when they are well established.** Anything I'd like you or a second reader to confirm is listed near the end.

**How the levels feel, in plain words**

- **g1** — 3 to 5 short sentences of mostly sound-it-out words (*Sam has a red hat. The wind is strong today.*) with a few everyday sight words. **She reads it all herself**: the passage, the question ("What took the hat?") and three choices of a word or two ("the wind · a dog · the rain"), with help on a word only if she asks.
- **g2** — 40–70 words, a small story with a problem and a fix, or a few true facts. She reads the questions herself. Three choices.
- **g3** — 80–120 words, one or two harder words explained right in the passage (*hinge, the metal joint that lets the gate swing*). Four choices, like the 3rd-grade test.
- **g4** — 120–160 words. More "why did she…?" and "what does this phrase mean here?" Four choices.
- **g5** — 150–200 words. Five choices, as in Ellis's and Lincoln's banks. Two new kinds of question begin: *why did the author include this?* and *which detail best supports this?*
- **g6** — 180–240 words, history and science as well as stories (the Pony Express, a lighthouse in a storm, the burr that became a fastener).
- **g78** — 220–300 words. A letter to save a park's climbing tree that answers the other side, a science-fair story where the guess is wrong, the Lost Colony of Roanoke, coral reefs. Same five-choice questions, harder thinking.

**Inside each level they go easier → harder** (the `o` number is the reading order), by the grade estimate and length; the new g1 and g2 passages are mixed in with the first ten, not tacked on the end. Wrong choices are taken from the passage itself or from common misreadings (the tadpole when the question asks what a frog *starts* as; "a kind of flower" for *the kite rose*; "mean and unfriendly" for *cold-blooded*). There is never an "all of the above".

**Page space** (decided: a full page for g6 and g7–8). g1–g4 fit the old half-page box.

**Comfortable, not hard.** Each level averages at or a little under its own grade on the estimate (see the table above). On the first check, 2nd grade averaged 2.4, a stretch for a child reading alone; those passages were rewritten in shorter sentences. The new g1 and g2 passages were held to the same mark. A child placed on a level should find most passages easy to get through; the questions are where the thinking happens.

**Stretch words.** Never more than two in a passage (the table counts them per level). Each is explained right in the sentence, so the word question can be answered from the page, never from what the child already happens to know.

**Getting ready for the test.** The question kinds are the ones on the reading tests at each age — find it, work it out, word meaning, main idea, and from 5th grade author's purpose and which detail supports a claim. Choices go 3 → 4 → 5 to match.

**Troubling drill items.** This ladder is fixed reading, so it does not follow each child's drill misses by itself. Two ways it can later: the notebook could pick a passage whose topic matches a deck in trouble (each passage now has `topics` tags, such as `frogs` or `snakes`), and a wrong answer here could feed the needs list by kind (for example, "word meaning" missed often). Neither is built; both are wiring decisions for later.

## Write-first days (from 3rd grade)

You chose "Write first some days." On those days the build will hide the choices — on paper they go on the back; on the tablet they stay hidden until something is written. For that, every question from g3 up carries a flag, `writeFirst`:

- **true** — the question still works with the choices hidden. The child writes an answer in their own words, and you can mark it against the *why* line ("What is a hinge?", "Why did the baby sleep late?", "What is the author's main purpose?").
- **false** — the stem only makes sense with the choices in front of the child: "What is the best title?", "Which detail best supports…?", "Which sentence best sums up…?".

I did not reword any stem to raise the count. Two questions whose answers are feelings ("Why does Simon speak quietly?", "How does the speaker feel about the mapmaker?") are marked false after the second read: written freely, several feelings are fair, so only the choices can be marked. Three "Which…" questions are marked true because a child can write them first and every right answer is on your page: "Which way do the hatchlings head?", "Which stars point the way to Polaris?", "Which tip is on their poster?" (any of the three tips counts).

## Two full samples from each level

The ✅ marks the right answer **here only**; on the child's page there is no mark. The *why* goes on your page. On the real pages the answer letters are spread evenly over A–E so the right one is never in the same spot. "Write first: yes" marks a question that can be shown without its choices on a write-first day.

### g1 — 1st grade

**The Red Hat** · story · 30 words · grade estimate -0.1 · topics: kids · `ra_g1_01`

> Sam has a red hat. The wind is strong today. It lifts the hat off his head and drops it in a big puddle! Sam picks it up and laughs.

1. *(find it)* What color is the hat?
   - A. red ✅
   - B. blue
   - C. green
   - *Why (parent page):* The first line: "Sam has a red hat."
2. *(find it)* What took the hat?
   - A. a dog
   - B. the rain
   - C. the wind ✅
   - *Why (parent page):* "The wind is strong today. It lifts the hat off his head."
3. *(work it out)* How does Sam feel at the end?
   - A. glad ✅
   - B. mad
   - C. sad
   - *Why (parent page):* "Sam picks it up and laughs." You laugh when you are glad, not mad or sad.

**Wild Ponies** · true facts · 35 words · grade estimate 1.3 · topics: animals, north-carolina · `ra_g1_18`

> Wild horses live on some islands by the sea in North Carolina. They run free, and no one rides them. They eat grass and drink from ponds. People must stay back and let them be.

1. *(find it)* Where do the horses live?
   - A. on islands by the sea ✅
   - B. in a red barn
   - C. on a farm
   - *Why (parent page):* "Wild horses live on some islands by the sea in North Carolina."
2. *(find it)* What do they eat?
   - A. fish
   - B. cake
   - C. grass ✅
   - *Why (parent page):* "They eat grass and drink from ponds."
3. *(work it out)* Why must people stay back?
   - A. The horses are wild. ✅
   - B. The horses are sick.
   - C. It is a game.
   - *Why (parent page):* They are "wild horses" that "run free," so people "let them be."

### g2 — 2nd grade

**Leo's Kite** · story · 54 words · grade estimate 1.7 · topics: kids · `ra_g2_03`

> Leo made a kite out of paper and sticks. He ran across the field, but the kite just bumped along the grass. Grandpa watched and said, "Try a longer tail." Leo tied on a long strip of cloth. This time the kite rose up and up until it looked as small as a bird.

1. *(word meaning)* "The kite rose up and up." What does rose mean here?
   - A. a kind of flower
   - B. went up ✅
   - C. fell down
   - *Why (parent page):* "Rose up and up until it looked as small as a bird" — it went higher and higher.
2. *(find it)* What did Leo tie on for a tail?
   - A. a long strip of cloth ✅
