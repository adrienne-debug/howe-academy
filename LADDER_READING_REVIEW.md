# Read and answer — a reading ladder for every kid (draft for review)

**Nothing here is loaded or live yet.** These are new files on a branch. No notebook prints them, no device sees them, and nothing was written to the family database. They are for you to read first, the way the boys' banks were.

This is the first of the five shared ladders in the notebooks study (§25, "units to develop" #1): **a short passage with a few questions**, in seven levels from 1st grade to 7th–8th. A child steps on at their level, keeps their place, and moves **up** at the end of a level instead of starting over. Every level written once serves every child after — Lucy, then Julian; Ellis climbing into what Lincoln reads; another family's 7th grader.

Why it matters now: Lucy's notebook has not one sentence to read, and her first test (May / summer 2027) has a reading part. Ellis lost his daily passage when his question bank replaced it. Lincoln gets one passage a week.

What I kept to, from your rules:
- **The answer never shows on the child's page.** Each question has the right letter and a one-line *why* for **your** page only — it points to the words in the passage that prove the answer.
- **Works printed and on a tablet.** Plain text, no pictures needed, no step that depends on covering anything. Circle a letter on paper; tap it on the tablet.
- **Any family can use it.** All writing is new (not copied from any book, test or workbook). No family names, no brands or characters — no snakes-and-Transformers theme. Topics: animals and nature, weather and space, how everyday things work, kids solving small problems, long-ago people and inventions, places.
- **About half stories, half true facts**, plus a how-to, a letter or notice, or a poem in most levels for variety (70 in all: 27 stories, 28 true-facts pieces, 6 how-tos, 5 poems, 4 letters or notices).
- **True facts only when they are well established.** Anything I'd like you or a second reader to confirm is listed near the end.

## What each level is like

| Level | Who it fits | Length | Questions | Choices | What the questions ask |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **g1** — 1st grade | Lucy now; Julian later | 3–5 short sentences (15–45 words) | 3 | 3 | Read aloud to her (by you or the tablet). Find it, a little work-it-out, what is it about. |
| **g2** — 2nd grade | Lucy, next year — the read-it-yourself step a year before the 3rd-grade test form | 40–70 words | 3 | 3 | Find it, work it out, one word meaning, main idea or best title. |
| **g3** — 3rd grade | the first year the test makes a child read alone | 80–120 words | 4 | 4 | Same four kinds, plus a stretch word explained in the passage. |
| **g4** — 4th grade | Ellis now (where his old passages were) | 120–160 words | 4 | 4 | Same, with more work-it-out and figurative words (a tree's "green jacket"). |
| **g5** — 5th grade | Ellis, next | 150–200 words | 4 | 5 | Adds author's purpose and which detail supports a claim — the older kids' test kinds. |
| **g6** — 6th grade | Lincoln now | 180–240 words | 4 | 5 | Same six kinds; longer, with history and science. |
| **g78** — 7th–8th grade | Lincoln next; another family's 7th grader | 220–300 words | 4 | 5 | Same six kinds; theme, counter-arguments in a letter, reading a claim against evidence. |

**How the levels feel, in plain words**

- **g1** — 3 to 5 short sentences of mostly sound-it-out words (*Sam has a red hat. The wind is strong today.*) with a few everyday sight words. The questions and choices are meant to be **read to her**; the passage she reads herself, with help as needed. Three short choices each.
- **g2** — 40–70 words, a small story with a problem and a fix, or a few true facts. She reads the questions herself. Three choices.
- **g3** — 80–120 words, one or two harder words explained right in the passage (*hinge, the metal joint that lets the gate swing*). Four choices, like the 3rd-grade test.
- **g4** — 120–160 words. More "why did she…?" and "what does this phrase mean here?" Four choices.
- **g5** — 150–200 words. Five choices, as in Ellis's and Lincoln's banks. Two new kinds of question begin: *why did the author include this?* and *which detail best supports this?*
- **g6** — 180–240 words, history and science as well as stories (the Pony Express, the printing press, a lighthouse in a storm).
- **g78** — 220–300 words. A persuasive letter with the other side answered, a science-fair story where the guess is wrong, railroad time, coral reefs. Same five-choice questions, harder thinking.

**Inside each level they go easier → harder**, by length and by the grade estimate below. Wrong choices are taken from the passage itself or from common misreadings (the tadpole when the question asks what a frog *starts* as; "a kind of flower" for *the kite rose*). There is never an "all of the above".

**Page space.** The old passages were 60–120 words with two questions. g1–g4 fit the same half page. A g6 or g78 passage with four five-choice questions fills most of a page — see question 6 at the end.

## Two full samples from each level

The ✅ marks the right answer **here only**; on the child's page there is no mark. The *why* goes on your page. (On the real pages the answer letters are spread evenly over A–E so the right one is never in the same spot.)

### g1 — 1st grade

**The Red Hat** · story · 30 words · grade estimate -0.1 · `ra_g1_01`

> Sam has a red hat. The wind is strong today. It lifts the hat off his head and drops it in a big puddle! Sam picks it up and laughs.

1. *(find it)* What color is Sam's hat?
   - A. red ✅
   - B. blue
   - C. green
   - *Why (parent page):* The first sentence says "Sam has a red hat."
2. *(find it)* What took the hat off Sam's head?
   - A. a dog
   - B. the wind ✅
   - C. the rain
   - *Why (parent page):* "The wind is strong today. It lifts the hat off his head."
3. *(work it out)* How does Sam feel at the end?
   - A. He is mad.
   - B. He thinks it is funny. ✅
   - C. He is sad.
   - *Why (parent page):* "Sam picks it up and laughs." People laugh when something is funny.

### g5 — 5th grade

**Counting the Thunder** · story · 169 words · grade estimate 4 · `ra_g5_05`

> The lights flickered once, then went out. In the sudden darkness, Mateo's little brother grabbed his arm, and outside, rain hammered against the windows. "It's okay, Sam," Mateo said, switching on a flashlight. "Want to know a trick for telling how far away the storm is?" Sam nodded. "When you see lightning, start counting seconds. Stop when you hear the thunder." A white flash lit the room. "One, two, three," they counted together. On eleven, the thunder rumbled. "Light travels much faster than sound," Mateo explained. "We see the flash almost right away, but the sound takes about five seconds to go one mile. Eleven seconds means the lightning was about two miles away." They waited for the next flash, and this time they counted to fifteen. After the one that followed, they reached nineteen. Slowly, Sam's grip on Mateo's arm loosened. "It's getting farther away," he said. "Exactly," said Mateo. By the time the lights blinked back on, Sam was asleep on the couch, still holding the flashlight.

1. *(find it)* About how long does the sound of thunder take to travel one mile?
   - A. about one second
   - B. about eleven seconds
   - C. about two minutes
   - D. no time at all
   - E. about five seconds ✅
   - *Why (parent page):* Mateo says "the sound takes about five seconds to go one mile."
2. *(work it out)* Why did Sam's grip on Mateo's arm loosen?
   - A. He felt less scared as the storm moved away. ✅
   - B. The flashlight was too heavy.
   - C. Mateo told him to let go.
   - D. The lights had come back on.
   - E. He wanted to count by himself.
   - *Why (parent page):* It loosens right after the counts grow to fifteen and nineteen, and Sam says "It's getting farther away." The lights come on later.
3. *(what supports this)* Which detail best shows that the storm was moving away?
   - A. Rain hammered the windows.
   - B. The counts went from eleven to fifteen to nineteen. ✅
   - C. The lights flickered once.
   - D. A white flash lit the room.
   - E. Light travels faster than sound.
   - *Why (parent page):* Longer counts mean the thunder took longer to arrive — so the lightning was farther off each time.
4. *(author's purpose)* Why does the author include Mateo's explanation about light and sound?
   - A. to make the storm seem scarier
   - B. to show how the counting trick works ✅
   - C. to explain why the lights went out
   - D. to tell how a flashlight works
   - E. to show that Mateo is bored
   - *Why (parent page):* Without the explanation, the reader would not know why counting seconds tells the distance.

**The Long Flight of the Monarchs** · true facts · 155 words · grade estimate 5.6 · `ra_g5_08`

> Every fall, millions of monarch butterflies in eastern North America begin an amazing trip. They fly south to spend the winter in the mountain forests of central Mexico. Some travel about 3,000 miles. There they cluster on fir trees. So many crowd together that branches can bend under their weight. What makes this journey remarkable is that none of the butterflies has ever made it before. Most monarchs live only a few weeks. But the ones born at the end of summer are different.
