## Summary (half a page)

**What number sense is.** It is not one skill. It is about seven separate skills ("strands") that grow side by side: saying the counting words in order; counting things and knowing that the last number tells "how many"; seeing small amounts without counting; reading and naming written numerals; comparing sizes (more, less, where a number sits on a line); breaking numbers into parts (5 is 3 and 2); and place value (the 4 in 42 means four tens). 📚 NRC 2009; Clements & Sarama; Math Recovery.

**Your question.** Yes, you are right: **naming a numeral and understanding tens and ones are different skills.** A child can read "42" and still think the 4 means four things. Naming comes first and can run a little ahead. Place value grows more slowly, from kindergarten to about 4th grade. Teach them side by side. Every new numeral comes with its tens-and-ones picture. But don't race the names far ahead of the meaning. 📚 Ross 1989; Mix et al. 2022; Common Core standards.

**What the app covers today.** 🔍 Counting to 20, naming numerals to 20, and the "Number of the Day" (to 100, drawn as tens and ones) are covered well for Julian. The thinking questions in the number ladder are good, but they are **switched off for every kid**. **Five things are missing or thin:**
1. seeing amounts at a glance (no quick-look practice anywhere);
2. making a set ("draw 7 dots"), not just counting one;
3. counting back, and counting on from any number;
4. number bonds / part–whole for Julian (the cards exist in the Math Fluency Path, but no kid is on that stage);
5. place value past 100, number lines, and big numbers for the older kids.

**Before Singapore Dimensions with Julian**, the strands that must be solid are counting, how-many, seeing small amounts, comparing, and part–whole to 5 and then 10. Naming numerals past 20 is nice, but it is not the gate. 📚 + 💡 A five-minute check a parent can run is in §3.

**The plan (§6)** is 10 small slices. Each one is useful on its own, and each keeps the rule that **notebooks complement drills**: the drill practises quick naming and recall, and the notebook asks the child to *make, show, place or explain*. First comes a five-minute "Where is this child?" check for any kid at any level. Then Julian's numerals past 20 (your ask), a quick-look drill, and a "make it" page. **12 questions for you are in §7**, each with my suggested answer.

## 1. Your question, answered first

> "Is recognising/naming the bigger numbers (30, 40 … 100, then 1,000, 10,000, 100,000) the same as understanding tens and ones? Are those both covered?"

### 1a. Two different skills — yes

| | **Naming a numeral** | **Understanding place value** |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Seeing **42** and saying "forty-two" (or hearing "forty-two" and pointing to 42) | Knowing the **4** means **4 tens = 40 things** and the **2** means 2 ones. Seeing 42 as "40 and 2", or as 3 tens and 12 ones |
| What it's like | Naming a letter. It's a link between a shape and a word | Knowing what a word *means*. It's an idea about groups |
| How it's learned | Repetition: flash cards, seeing numbers every day | Building and breaking groups of ten with real things, then pictures, then numerals |
| Usual timing | Recognising 1–5, then 6–10, in Pre-K/K; 0–20 in K; read and write to 120 in 1st; to 1,000 in 2nd; to 1,000,000 in 4th 📚 (Dimensions scope; Common Core) | Teens as "ten and some more" in K; tens and ones to 100 in 1st; hundreds in 2nd; each place ten times the next in 4th; decimals in 4th–5th 📚 (Common Core K.NBT.1 → 5.NBT.1) |
| Research | In a study of 279 children, kindergartners' "which number is bigger" knowledge of written numbers and their real tens-and-ones knowledge were **separate skills** 📚 Mix et al. 2022 | The "face value" mix-up: a child reads 52 but thinks the 5 means five things. Many 4th–5th graders still had it 📚 Ross 1989. The classic check is to ask the child to show with objects what each digit means (Kamii's version uses 16 chips: the original percentages weren't verified online) |

### 1b. Should they be taught side by side?

Yes, with naming a little ahead. 💡 That matches the standards' order 📚 (Common Core K.CC → K.NBT.1 → 1.NBT) and Fuson's point that a child must move from counting ones to treating ten as one unit 📚 (Fuson et al. 1997). Every time a child meets a new decade name, show it as tens: "forty = 4 tens". The trap is letting the **names run far ahead of the meaning**. A 4-year-old can learn to say "ten thousand", but the word stands for nothing yet. That's harmless as a fun fact. As a drill, though, it teaches him that numbers are just words to recite.

### 1c. The known traps

📚 Fuson; Miura; Miller & Stigler; Kamii. Each trap comes with a 💡 fix.

| Trap | What happens | What helps 💡 |
|---|---|---|
| **The teens** | "Eleven" and "twelve" have no pattern. "Four**teen**" says the 4 first, so children write **41** for 14 📚 (Common Core progression, p. 16) | Teens as "ten and 4 more", always with a full ten-frame plus extras. Saying "ten-four" sometimes can help. East Asian number words name the ten first, and Japanese and Korean first graders built numbers with tens far more often 📚 (Miura 1993). But words alone don't do it: most Chinese 4-year-olds still didn't see teens as ten and ones 📚 (Ho & Fuson 1998), so the picture matters |
| **Thirteen / thirty** | They sound almost the same. Kids mix them up hearing *and* reading (💡 widely reported by teachers; I found no study) | Pair them on purpose: 13 = one ten and 3; 30 = three tens. Show both pictures side by side |
| **"-ty" hides the ten** | "Forty" doesn't say "four tens" | Say both: "forty — four tens" (the app already prints "4 tens and 2 ones" 🔍) |
| **Crossing a decade** | "…28, 29, twenty-ten", or stopping at 29 📚 (children usually go on once told the next decade word) | Practise just the crossings: 29→30, 39→40, 99→100 |
| **Writing what you hear** | "Six hundred forty-two" written as **600402**. This is a normal step, not a mistake to worry about 📚 (Byrge, Smith & Mix 2014) | Hundreds only after tens are solid; build 642 as 6 hundreds, 4 tens, 2 ones |
| **Big numbers with no size** | Reading 10,000 fluently with no idea it's ten 1,000s, or that it's a big crowd | Every big number shown as "10 of the last one"; real-life benchmarks (a full stadium) |
| **Reading 42 but not "seeing" 42** | Thinks 42 is "a 4 and a 2" | The tens-and-ones picture, "build it" pages, and number lines |

## 2. What number sense is — the strands, in plain words

**Why it matters** 📚. Math skill at school entry was the strongest predictor of later school achievement in six large long-term studies, ahead of early reading and attention (Duncan et al. 2007). That is a link, not proof of cause. The counting skills that predicted 5th-grade math best were the **"advanced" ones**: counting on from a number, counting back, and knowing "how many". Plain reciting predicted less (Nguyen et al. 2016). The NRC report says nearly every young child can become competent in math if given the chance, and that number deserves the most time from ages 3 to 6 (NRC 2009).

**One honest caution** 📚. Gains from early math programs often shrink over the next few years (Bailey et al. 2016). The government's practice guide rates "teach number in a developmental order" as **moderate** evidence, not strong (Frye et al. 2013). The order below is the best map we have. Use it as a map, not a rulebook.

### The eight strands

Ages are rough. Children vary a lot, and the standards (what schools *expect*) are not norms (what is *common*).

| # | Strand, plain words | The usual order a child grows through | Roughly when | What the research says |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Counting words**: saying the numbers in order | Says number words with no set order → a set list to 5, then 10 → counts **back from 10** → counts **on from any number** ("start at 6") → to 100 by ones and tens → to 120 from any start → skip counting (2s, 5s, 10s) | List to ~20 by age 5; to 100 by end of K; to 120 by end of 1st | 📚 Clements & Sarama counting trajectory; Common Core K.CC.1–2, 1.NBT.1; Head Start expects 20 by age 5. Children often stop at 29, 39, 49 and continue once told the next decade word (the "-ty" rule) |
| 2 | **One-to-one and "how many"** (cardinality) | One word per object in a short row → "one-knower", "two-knower", "three-knower" … → **knows the last number said tells how many** → **makes a set**: "give me 7" (harder than counting one that's there) → counts 20 in a row, 10 scattered | Cardinality ~3½–4½; to 20 by end of K | 📚 Gelman & Gallistel's five counting principles (1978); Wynn's "Give-N" knower levels (summarised in Le Corre; Lee & Sarnecka 2011); K.CC.4–5. Rows are easiest, scattered things hardest. Parents talking about sets of **4–10 things you can see** predicted later understanding (Gunderson & Levine 2011) |
| 3 | **Seeing small amounts without counting** (subitizing) | Sees 1–3, then up to 4–5, at a glance (**perceptual**) → sees bigger amounts as known parts, e.g. 7 as 5 and 2 or 3 and 4 (**conceptual**) → five-frame and ten-frame "anchors" of 5 and 10 | Some at age 3, most before 5; conceptual in K–1st | 📚 IES subitizing progression (from Baroody and Clements & Sarama); Clements 1999; Van de Walle's four relationships (spatial patterns, one/two more and less, **anchors of 5 and 10**, part-part-whole). Van de Walle warns that ten-frames are hard at first, because many children count every dot |
| 4 | **Numerals**: recognising, naming, writing | Recognises 1–3, then 4–5 → writes 1–5 → 6–10 → 0–20 and matches a numeral to a set → reads and writes to 120 (1st) → to 1,000 (2nd) → to 1,000,000 (4th) | To 20 in K | 📚 Dimensions Math scope and sequence (KA ch. 2–3); K.CC.3, 1.NBT.1, 2.NBT.3, 4.NBT.2. Backward digits are common and usually normal under about age 7 (practitioner source). "Fourteen" written as 41 is widely reported (Common Core progression, p. 16) |
| 5 | **Comparing and sizes**: more/less, order, number line, estimating | Compares groups by matching, then by counting; learns "looks bigger" can mislead → compares written numbers 1–10 → "how many more?" (1st) → **number-line placement becomes even**: 0–100 by about 2nd grade, 0–1,000 by about 4th, 0–100,000 by about 6th | K → 6th | 📚 Booth & Siegler 2006; Thompson & Opfer; Laski & Yu 2013. **A 1-to-10 race board game played four times for 15 minutes closed the gap** between low-income and middle-income preschoolers. A circular board did not help (Siegler & Ramani 2008, 2009; replicated by Whyte & Bull 2008). Comparing **real numerals** links to math far more than comparing dot clouds (Schneider et al. 2017 meta-analysis) |
| 6 | **Parts and wholes** (number bonds) | Sees 4 as 2 and 2 → breaks 5, then 10, into parts in more than one way → knows the partner for 10 of any number → teens as 10 + n → **make-a-ten** (8 + 6 = 8 + 2 + 4 = 10 + 4) | Bonds to 5 in Pre-K/K, to 10 by end of K, make-a-ten in 1st | 📚 Common Core K.OA.3–5 and the progression, pp. 7–16; Van de Walle's part-part-whole is Singapore's **number bond**. Concrete → picture → numeral (CPA) comes from Bruner and is Singapore's backbone (secondary sources) |
| 7 | **Place value**: tens and ones and up | Teens = a ten and some ones (K) → two digits = tens and ones, ten as one bundle (1st) → hundreds (2nd, to 1,000) → each place is ten times the one to its right (4th, to 1,000,000) → decimals to thousandths (5th) | K → 5th, and often shaky into 4th–5th | 📚 K.NBT.1, 1.NBT.2, 2.NBT.1, 4.NBT.1, 5.NBT.1. **Face value** mix-up: reads 52 but thinks the 5 means five things. Many 4th–5th graders still had it (Ross 1989). In kindergarten, "which number is bigger" knowledge and real tens-and-ones knowledge were **separate skills** (Mix et al. 2022). Young children writing 600402 for "six hundred forty-two" is a normal step (Byrge, Smith & Mix 2014). Japanese and Korean first graders built numbers with tens far more than U.S. ones (Miura 1993), but most Chinese 4-year-olds still didn't use teens as ten-and-ones despite clear number words (Ho & Fuson 1998). Teaching matters, not just language |
| 8 | **Where it leads**: adding, fractions, decimals | Counts all → **counts on** → uses known facts and make-a-ten (Math Recovery's stages: emergent, perceptual, figurative, counting on/back, …, facile) → fractions as **numbers on the same line** → decimals as more places | 1st → 6th | 📚 Wright, Martland & Stafford (*Early Numeracy*); Common Core progression; Siegler, Thompson & Schneider 2011: one number line runs through whole numbers and fractions, and "1/8 is bigger than 1/4" comes from whole-number habits. The IES fractions guide puts number lines at the centre (moderate evidence). Weak place value is a main cause of decimal trouble later (Ladel et al. 2023) |

### Where the evidence is thin or argued about 📚

- **Dot-cloud "gut sense" games** (guess which cloud has more): the early excitement (Halberda et al. 2008) has faded. A review found no good evidence that training this sense improves real arithmetic (Szűcs & Myers 2017). Practise with **real numerals, number lines and counting** instead. Note this is *not* the same as subitizing small exact amounts (strand 3), which is in every early curriculum.
- **Montessori**: overall positive effects in a large review (Randolph et al. 2023), but math effects were weaker than reading in the newest national study. No rigorous study looks at the golden beads on their own.
- **Base-ten blocks are not magic.** In one study, training preschoolers with written symbols helped more than training with blocks (Mix et al., MSU summary). Kamii warned against drilling place value before children think in groups.
- **Number Talks** (short class talks about how you solved it): promising, but evidence is small classroom studies with no comparison groups.
- **How much rote counting matters**: some reciting is needed, but the studies point to counting on, counting back and "how many" as the parts that predict later math. All of them are correlational.
- **Big numbers** (thousands to millions): I found no solid research on how children come to sense their size, beyond the number-line studies. That's a real gap. The plan's big-number pages are 💡 my suggestion, built on the "10 of the last one" idea from the standards.

## 3. Before Singapore Dimensions: what must be solid, and a five-minute check

### What Dimensions covers 📚 (Singapore Math Inc. scope and sequence, 2019)

| Book | Number topics |
|---|---|
| **PK A/B** (about age 4) | Count 1–5, "how many do you see?", numbers to 10, zero, count back, order numbers, more/fewer, **"what's the other part?"**, add and subtract within 5 |
| **K A** | Numbers to 5 (count, recognise 1–3 then 4–5, write 1–5, zero), numbers to 10, one more than, comparing within 10 |
| **K B** | Numbers to 20 ("ten and some more"), **number bonds 2–10**, adding (count on, to 10), taking away within 10, numbers to 100 (count by tens and fives) |
| **1A** | Numbers to 10 again, then **number bonds (make 6 … make 10)** in chapter 2, add and subtract with bonds and counting on/back, numbers to 20, **adding by making 10** |
| **1B** | Numbers to 40, then to 100, as tens and ones |
| **2A** | Numbers to 1,000: hundreds, tens and ones |

### What must be solid before starting 💡 (from the scope above and the research in §2)

| Starting… | Must be solid | Nice, not required |
|---|---|---|
| **Dimensions K** | Says the numbers to 10; counts up to 10 things one-to-one and answers "how many?"; sees 1–3 at a glance | Naming numerals past 10 (K teaches 1–10 itself) |
| **Dimensions 1A** | Counts to 20 and back from 10; counts on from any number to 20; "how many" to 10 (and makes a set of up to 10); sees 1–5 at a glance and 6–10 on a ten-frame as "5 and some"; reads and writes 0–20; one more / one less; which is more within 10; **breaks 5 into parts in more than one way**, starting on 10; teens as "ten and some more" | Numerals 21–100 (1B teaches them) |

**For Julian specifically** 💡: he already names numerals to 18–20 and sees 21–100 in his notebook. That's ahead of Dimensions K. The gaps that matter for Singapore are **counting back, counting on from any number, making a set, seeing small amounts at a glance, and number bonds to 5**. That's why slices 3–6 matter more than going to 1,000.

### The five-minute check a parent can run 💡 (built from the tasks used in the research)

You need 20 pennies (or cereal pieces), a pencil, and this list. Say each ask out loud, without helping. Tick **easy**, **working on it** or **not yet**. Stop when he's tired. The *Little* check is for Pre-K to 1st.

| # | Ask (you say it) | Strand | "Easy" looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Count for me as high as you can." | Counting words | Pre-K: to 10+; K-ready: 20; 1st-ready: 100, with 29 → 30 right |
| 2 | "Start at 6 and keep counting." Then: "Count backward from 10." | Counting on / back | Starts at 6 without going back to 1; reaches 0 or 1 going back |
| 3 | Put 15 pennies out: "Give me 7." Then: "How many did you give me?" | Making a set; "how many" | Stops at 7. Says "7" without counting again |
| 4 | Lay 5 pennies in a dice pattern, let him look for about 2 seconds, sweep your hand over them: "How many?" Try 3, then 4, then 5 | Seeing amounts at a glance | Says it without pointing or counting |
| 5 | Write 3, 8, 12, 15, 19 (later 30, 42, 67). Point: "What number is this?" | Naming numerals | Names each one quickly |
| 6 | "Which is more, 6 or 9?" (no pennies). "What comes after 7? Before 7?" | Comparing; one more / less | Answers without counting from 1 |
| 7 | Show 5 pennies: "Put them in two piles. How many here? How many there? Can you make two piles a different way?" | Parts and wholes | Finds two ways (e.g. 4 and 1, 3 and 2) |
| 8 | Lay 10 pennies in two rows of five and 4 more beside them: "How many?" | Teens as ten and more | Says "ten… fourteen" (counts on from 10), not from 1 |

The **Big** check (2nd–6th) uses the same idea, in under five minutes:

| # | Ask | Strand | "Easy" looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Write 36. Put out 36 pennies in a pile: "Show me what the 3 means." | Place value (face value check 📚 Ross; Kamii) | Moves 30 pennies, not 3 |
| 2 | "Count by tens from 73." Then "back by tens from 142." | Counting off the decade | 83, 93, 103 …; 132, 122 … |
| 3 | Draw a line, label 0 and 100: "Put a mark where 47 goes." (Older: 0 to 1,000 and 750; 0 to 1 and ¾) | Sizes on a line | Close to the right spot |
| 4 | "Read this": 4,306 and 30,405 | Reading big numbers | Reads them, zeros and all |
| 5 | "What is 10 more than 3,995? 100 more?" | Place value with bigger numbers | 4,005; 4,095 |
| 6 | "Which is bigger, 0.45 or 0.5?" (4th+) | Decimals' place value | 0.5, and can say why |
| 7 | "About how much is 49 + 52?" | Estimating | "About 100", without working it out |

What to do with the result: each "not yet" names its strand, and §6 says which slice works on it.

## 5. The rules every slice keeps 💡

These are your standing rules, applied to number sense:

1. **Drill ≠ notebook.** The drill (Mastery, tablet) does *quick naming and recall*. The notebook does *make, show, place, explain*. The same number can appear in both, but never in the same form.
2. **Printed and tablet.** Every notebook item works with a pencil on paper and with the pen on the tablet page (🔍 `NBTABLET` has pen / erase / undo). No step relies on covering, folding or hiding an answer. A "quick look" with a timer lives only in the drill, because paper can't time it.
3. **The app never supplies the answer.** The page gives the *numeral* and the child makes the amount, or it gives the *amount* and the child writes the numeral, never both. Answers print only on the Grown-Up Guide / Companion, as now.
4. **Alone, except the littlest.** Pre-K/K pages are read aloud by a grown-up or by the tablet's 🔊. From 1st grade up, one short line a child can read.
5. **A first-year homeschool mom can run it.** Each page has a one-line "what to look for" in the Grown-Up Guide, with no teaching words.
6. **One home for each control.** One "🔢 Number sense" card per kid on the Notebook tab (on/off, level), and the drill stages stay on the Math Fluency Path. Dad sees and uses the same card.
7. **Nothing of one family leaks.** All items are made by rule from numbers, dots, shapes and the kid's own counting picture. No names, no themes from another family.
